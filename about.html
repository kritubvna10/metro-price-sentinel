Overview

What is the Price Sentinel Network?

The Metro Vancouver Price Sentinel Network monitors grocery prices across Metro Vancouver and tells families two things: which stores are charging more than the government says groceries should cost, and exactly how much extra low-income families pay every year just because they cannot afford to buy in bulk.

BC has a food insecurity rate of 24.4 percent. Grocery costs have risen 27 percent over five years. But those headline numbers do not tell a South Asian family in Surrey which store to shop at this week. They do not tell a Korean family in Burnaby that they are paying $1,319 more per year than their neighbor who shops at Costco. This project does.

Methodology

The Two Core Metrics

Store Premium Score

Premium = (Store Price − StatCan Price) ÷ StatCan Price × 100

A positive score means the store charges more than the BC provincial average. A negative score means it is cheaper. This is anchored to the official BC Consumer Price Index (Statistics Canada Table 18-10-0245-01) so it is not an opinion — it is a comparison against a government number.

StatCan baselines used: Staples $4.89 · Produce $3.42 · Protein $9.18 · Dairy $5.64

The Poverty Penalty

Penalty = Small Package Total − Bulk Package Total (per bi-weekly basket)

This answers a question nobody else is asking: how much extra money does a family pay just because they cannot afford to buy in bulk? The pilot data shows this ranges from $19.26 to $51.22 per bi-weekly shopping trip. That is between $500 and $1,319 per year. Same food. Same store. Same family. The only difference is whether they had enough cash to buy the big bag.

Data & Research

The Data

Seven culturally specific grocery baskets built from peer-reviewed research: South Asian, Chinese, Filipino, Korean, European, Indigenous, and Others.

10 stores across Metro Vancouver. 1,308 rows of real price data collected April–May 2026 from store websites. 7 demographic personas. 2 package sizes (small and bulk) for each product.

Basket Key Research Decision Source
Chinese Uses soy milk instead of dairy milk because East Asian lactose intolerance prevalence is 70–90%. PMC9147302
South Asian Prioritizes garlic over ginger because garlic appears in every South Asian savory dish — dal, curry, sabzi. Dietary literature
Filipino Uses yellow onions as a core item because they appear in literally every Filipino dish including adobo, sinigang, and kare-kare. Culinary research
Korean Includes mackerel because it is the most consumed fish in Korean households. The original dataset had zero fish. KHANES survey data
Indigenous Uses shortening instead of cooking oil because bannock — the number one staple food — requires shortening or lard. FNFNES 2008–2016
Community Guides

How to Use This Dashboard

New Immigrants & Newcomer Families

The cheapest store overall in your city is often not the cheapest store for your specific community. A store that is great value for a general Canadian basket might be significantly more expensive for South Asian or Filipino staples like atta, dal, tilapia, and rice.

Use the dashboard to filter by your community and find which store in your neighbourhood is actually cheapest for the food your family eats. If you can afford to buy in bulk even occasionally, it saves real money. The gap between a 2 kg bag of rice and a 10 kg bag is roughly $5 per month on rice alone.

Students & Young People

Use the dashboard to find which store near you is below the government baseline this week. That means it is literally cheaper than what Statistics Canada says groceries should cost in BC.

The poverty penalty is real — if you are buying small packages because of budget or space constraints, split a bulk purchase with a roommate. The demographic filter exists for a reason: if you are from a Filipino, South Asian, Korean, Chinese, or Indigenous background, your grocery basket looks different from the StatCan average. The dashboard accounts for that.

Community Organizations & Food Banks

The dashboard shows which stores are cheapest for specific communities this week. It shows the poverty penalty in dollar terms so you can explain to families concretely how much they could save.

It shows which stores are above the government baseline so you have factual, government-anchored evidence for advocacy work. The data is freely available. Everything updates weekly.

Case Study

The Poverty Penalty in Numbers

Maria's Story — Surrey, BC

Maria is a Filipino Canadian mother of three living in Surrey. She shops bi-weekly because that is when she gets paid. She buys white rice in 2 kg bags because she does not have $18 available at once for a 10 kg bag, even though the 10 kg bag would save her money over the month.

The Price Sentinel data shows that for a Filipino family basket at stores in Surrey, the poverty penalty ranges from $30 to $40 per bi-weekly shop. Over a year that is between $780 and $1,040 in extra spending. Not because Maria is making bad decisions. Because she does not have the cash buffer to buy in bulk.

This is not a personal finance problem. It is a structural inequity built into how grocery pricing works. The Price Sentinel Network makes it visible in dollar terms for the first time in Metro Vancouver.
